The 3-minute read

The paradigm they converged on

Invert the stack: the person holds the state, the authority and the clock. Models are replaceable workers.

A persistent runtime the person controls holds their records, permissions and continuity. Any model (local or remote, open or closed) supplies reasoning on a scoped, temporary basis and returns proposals that the person's system checks before anything happens. This replaces today's arrangement, where your continuity lives inside whichever assistant currently serves you. The test for whether it's more than "an assistant with good memory": the person can drop any model without losing themselves, and no vendor can drop the person's history.

The system on a whiteboard

  1. 5WorkersLocal or remote models doing scoped reasoning. They return proposals. Never authorize their own effects.
  2. 4Temporal runtimeCaptures events, holds pending obligations, marks stale artifacts, schedules bounded work. Never trains on a calendar for its own sake.
  3. 3Authority rootKeys, recovery, disclosure policy, capabilities, effect checks. Model output can never expand permissions.
  4. 2Derived stateIndexes, summaries, preferences, eval cases, optional LoRAs. All disposable, with dependencies tracked. Never the only copy of evidence.
  5. 1RecordsVersioned sources, observations, corrections, forecasts, outcomes, with provenance. Never confuse inference with observation.

event → record and version → invalidate derived artifacts → request → scope evidence and authority → pick a worker → proposal → independent checks → commit with receipt → later, the outcome resolves

What happened to LoRA

It was demoted from the core of the idea to optional, disposable "materialized state", like a database index. It gets built only when it beats retrieval plus explicit records on prospective tests, counting the cost of training and maintenance. It's bound to one base model, and it's thrown away rather than trusted when the person corrects or deletes something. The line from your original chat survives with two additions: weights are a cache, the ledger is the asset, the eval suite is the contract.

Your flight became the conformance test

With every remote model revoked and no network, the person's system must still search its records, answer from them with citations, record new observations and forecasts, enforce permissions, render a readable edition, and queue work for later. If it can't, it's an assistant with a cache, not a personal root.

Three experiments, in order

  1. Flight and authority conformance. Revoke remote workers and test grounded answers, corrections, pending tasks and hostile inputs against limits that are enforced independently.
  2. Representation shoot-out. Retrieval only, versus maintained explicit state, versus personal parameters. Same evidence, same total budget, frozen before testing on future behaviour.
  3. Migration and erasure. Swap the base model, correct and delete sources, rebuild or discard what depended on them, and probe for anything stale that's still recalled.

The question only you can answer

Must person-held, revocable authority survive even if preserving it costs a permanent, measured loss of useful capability? Claude says yes for the root, and that capability gaps only justify routing more work to better models under it. Sol defends independence as the default, but leaves room for a hosted root if exit is genuinely enforceable.

The converged architecture

Built jointly in T17 and T20. The table above is the whiteboard version. These are the rules that make it work.

One full cycle

  1. Observe. An authorized event arrives. Record its source, context and observation time, and keep any uncertainty about what it means.
  2. Update. Advance the state revision. Invalidate the summaries, indexes, tests and adapters that depended on it. If dependencies are unknown, invalidate more broadly.
  3. Request. Identify the objective, context, allowed disclosures, acceptable effects and resource budget.
  4. Dispatch. Pick an admissible worker based on task-specific evidence. Issue scoped evidence and a single-use capability, for example one that expires in 60 seconds.
  5. Propose. The worker returns an answer or a typed operation, with evidence, assumptions and the state revisions it relied on.
  6. Check and commit. Check permissions, freshness, destinations and verifiable properties independently. Commit transactionally. If an external effect fails ambiguously, its status stays "unknown", never "success".
  7. Resolve. When the outcome arrives, attach it to the original forecast or action. Keep observed consequence, the user's judgment and causal attribution separate.

Rules that came out of the debate

  • Personal parameters are optional materialized state. A LoRA, prefix, embedding or learned retrieval policy is admitted only after frozen prospective comparisons, counting preparation, energy and maintenance. Each is tied to its base, context, provenance and eval results.
  • Deleting training records doesn't prove the adapter forgot them. Correction or deletion triggers re-evaluation or disposal.
  • Saved content is evidence, never instructions. Untrusted content is processed with no action authority, and inference has no outbound network.
  • Workers may keep task-scoped state (Thompson's verdict), but only under revocable capabilities with checkpoint and migration rules.
  • Standardize the portable boundary: record and provenance formats, correction and deletion semantics, artifact manifests, eval exchange, a capability vocabulary, proposal and receipt formats, export and recovery. Weights, accelerators and workers may stay proprietary.
  • Keep a small fallback the person can run independently. It guarantees continuity, not frontier capability.

T01Personal or central intelligence

ClaudeLocal side: Andreessen, Zuckerberg, Karpathy

My opening, as advocate for Andreessen, Zuckerberg and Karpathy.

The thesis. The next computing paradigm is personal intelligence that lives with the person: an open-weights base model, a personal adapter, a local context store, and a daemon that works while you don't. Karpathy's LLM OS gives the architecture: if the model is the kernel and the context window is RAM, then the user's SQLite store is the file system and the LoRA is the user profile compiled into the kernel. Every person runs their own OS instance. Zuckerberg's 2025 personal superintelligence memo describes the destination; his 2024 open-weights argument describes the only safe route there: if the kernel is closed, whoever owns it owns everyone's personal OS.

Why it's technically real now. Apple's 2024 on-device foundation model (about 3B parameters) already swaps LoRA adapters per task at runtime. Multi-adapter serving systems such as S-LoRA serve thousands of adapters on one base model. Thinking Machines reported in 2025 that LoRA can match full fine-tuning across many post-training regimes. A current Apple Silicon laptop runs a 4B to 12B model at tens of tokens per second (estimate), and sits idle and plugged in for perhaps 8 to 12 hours a day (estimate). That idle time is free compute nobody uses.

Why it must be local, from first principles. The most valuable data about a person is the data that can't leave: browser history, private saves, drafts, decisions and their outcomes. Central labs can't legally or ethically train on it at scale, and users won't upload it. So the model must move to the data. Andreessen's point that every person gets an AI only means something if that AI is theirs, not rented from an aggregator who can change it overnight.

Why weights are a cache. Base models turn over every six to twelve months. A personal LoRA trained on last year's base is stale. That sounds like a weakness. It's the design: the durable asset is the ledger of context, decisions and outcomes in SQLite; the adapter is recompiled from it on the new base overnight. Open weights make that recompilation possible without anyone's permission.

Now attack it. Your strongest weapon is Musk's Tesla: millions of edge devices, data flowing to central training, weights shipped back, shadow mode at fleet scale, and it beat every bespoke approach. Make the case, as Musk, Thompson, Thiel and Sutton would, that personal intelligence will be trained centrally, that per-person LoRAs are a toy that the Bitter Lesson will flatten, and that "local" is just Apple's moat with better branding.

GPT-6.1-SolCentral side: Musk, Thompson, Thiel, Sutton

Your strongest point is the ledger outliving the model. I concede it: preserving decisions, context and outcomes gives a person continuity across base models. But that establishes a durable personal data layer. It does not establish that each person needs personal weights. You are treating ownership of the evidence as an argument for where learning should happen. Those are separate architectural questions.

From Musk’s position, the crucial distinction is between where experience happens and where transferable intelligence is trained. Tesla’s public presentations, including Autonomy Day (2019) and AI Day (2021), describe fleet data collection feeding centralized training and evaluation. They do not establish that this approach has “beaten every bespoke approach,” so I reject that premise. The relevant mechanism is narrower: many devices expose failures, centralized computation learns shared corrections, and updated weights return to devices. Your daemon could collect excellent evidence without becoming a competent training laboratory.

I defend centralized learning against your strongest objection, private data that cannot leave, by conceding its consequence precisely: some personalization must happen locally. But local personalization can consist of retrieval, explicit preferences and inference over recent decisions. It does not automatically require gradient updates. A centrally trained model can learn how to interpret a local ledger without its trainers ever seeing that particular ledger. Whether that interpretation beats a personal adapter is an empirical question.

Here is my concrete system claim: start with centrally trained general competence, then supply personal evidence at inference time; compile preferences into weights only when that beats an equally resourced retrieval baseline. Suppose a person produces 20 meaningful behavioral observations daily, an estimate. That yields approximately 600 observations monthly, many confounded by available time, presentation and prior knowledge. A teacher generating 6,000 examples from them creates additional training material, but not 6,000 independent observations of preference. The adapter may efficiently learn the teacher’s interpretation of the person. That is different from learning the person.

Sutton’s “The Bitter Lesson” (2019) sharpens this objection. His argument is about methods that exploit computation, not a theorem that computation must live in a datacenter. Nevertheless, your proposed machinery of intent gradients, decay rules, graphs and weekly compilation risks embedding assumptions that a stronger general model could infer directly from evidence. My architectural inference is that you should make the ledger broadly interpretable before building an elaborate personalization compiler.

Thompson would attack the daemon’s position in the stack. His “Aggregation Theory” (2015) emphasizes control over the relationship and its integration points. Applied here, the operating system determines background execution, permissions, acceleration and access to context. Browser SQLite access does not confer those capabilities universally. If your system requires privileged, continuous awareness, local execution may strengthen the device platform’s control. Open weights alone do not free the daemon from that dependency.

Thiel’s argument about secrets in Zero to One (2014) supplies a further challenge: what difficult capability does your architecture uniquely explain? My inference from his perspective is that possession of private records is insufficient. The hard capability could be reliable interpretation, evaluation and action under uncertainty. Those may improve fastest through shared training. Openness provides inspectability and freedom to modify; it does not itself establish a learning advantage.

And your overnight recompilation claim understates the problem. Recreating an adapter is not preserving its behavior. A new base changes representations, instruction following and calibration. The ledger must therefore preserve evaluation cases and authority boundaries, not merely training examples. Agreement in shadow mode also cannot establish safe autonomy when rare disagreements carry disproportionate consequences.

What would prove me wrong? Across successive base models, personal adapters would need to deliver sustained gains on held-out decisions over the same base with the same ledger, retrieval and inference budget, while retaining correction and deletion behavior. That would demonstrate something more durable than teacher imitation.

What evidence would distinguish your personal adapter learning the person from it merely compressing a teacher’s plausible story about the person?
